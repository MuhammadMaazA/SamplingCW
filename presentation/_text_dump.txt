=== Slide 1: Error Correction over a Screen-to-Camera Optical Channel
  [Text 0] Muhammad Maaz and Kanav Mehta / COMP0228 Coursework 1, University College London
  NOTES (75 words): Maaz

We are Maaz and Kanav, and we will take turns presenting, one slide each. Our project is an optical link that sends bits from one laptop screen to another through an iPad. We tested whether basic error correcting codes can cope with the noise on this link. In the dim room that noise arrives in bursts, so the order in which we send the bits matters as much as the number of extra bits.

This slide shows what each laptop does, with the transmitter above and the receiver below. Our message is 16 bits. We add a CRC, short for cyclic redundancy check, a check value, which makes 32 bits, and the code adds extra bits to reach 96. With the sync word and the header, sent three times, this gives 48 plus 192 plus 96, one frame of 336 bits, and at 8 bps, meaning bits per second, it takes 42 seconds. In the bottom row, the webcam gives 23 to 30 camera frames a second, and for each one we keep the median brightness inside our box. Brighter than the threshold means a 1. The receiver then finds the sync word, even with up to 9 of its 48 bits wrong, votes on the header, and decodes the payload, checking both CRCs.

The number on the right is the code rate, which is the share of the sent bits that carry the message. Uncoded transmission has a rate of 1. This slide shows our three changes. The left charts show frames delivered, and the right charts show channel BER. In the first panel the speed rises from 3 to 8 bits per second, and the frames delivered fall from 14 of 21 to 9 of 21. The likely reason is that at 8 bits per second there are only about 4 samples per bit, but the two sessions were on different days, so speed may not be the only difference.
